It's a direct consequence of how heat moves through meat and how a temperature sensor actually samples it — a mechanism that placement rules alone don't address, because perfect placement doesn't produce a single "true" number. It produces a number that's accurate for one small point in a piece of food that doesn't have a uniform internal temperature at all.

## Why the same cut gives different numbers

Meat cooks from the outside in. At any moment before the center catches up, there's a temperature gradient running from the exterior to the core — hotter a few millimeters under the crust, cooler toward the middle. A probe doesn't measure "the meat's temperature"; it measures the temperature of whatever tissue sits against its sensing element, which for most instant-read and leave-in probes is a band of a few millimeters near the tip. Move that tip half an inch in any direction inside a roast that still has an active gradient, and you're sampling a genuinely different point on that gradient. The reading changes because the underlying physical quantity changed, not because the thermometer is inconsistent.

Local composition compounds this. Fat conducts heat differently than lean muscle, and muscle fiber orientation changes how quickly heat moves along versus across the grain. A probe tip that lands a few millimeters into a pocket of intramuscular fat, or parallel to the fiber direction instead of across it, is sitting in a micro-environment with different thermal conductivity than the spot an inch away — even though both points are, technically, "the thickest part, away from bone." Marbling isn't distributed evenly, so this effect varies cut to cut and even within the same cut. None of this means one reading is wrong. Both are locally accurate; they're just describing different micro-locations inside a piece of food that hasn't finished equalizing.

## Which reading to trust

When two correctly placed readings disagree, act on the lower one. Heat conducts inward from the surface toward the center, which makes the geometric center of the cut the last point to reach any given temperature and, by definition, the coolest point at any moment before the whole piece equalizes. A probe reading that comes in lower than a nearby reading is, on average, sitting closer to that true thermal center or in a zone that's lagging behind. If the coolest accessible point has reached the target temperature, everything around it — which is by definition hotter — has already cleared that bar. Averaging two readings, or trusting whichever number is higher, can tell you a cut is done when its actual center hasn't caught up. This is also why USDA minimums (165°F for poultry, 160°F for ground meats, 145°F with a three-minute rest for whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, and veal) are specified as the temperature the coldest point must reach, not an average across the cut.

Don't chase a single peak number either. Sliding a probe around while watching for the highest digit it'll produce finds a hot pocket near the surface or near a bone (bone conducts heat differently than surrounding tissue and often reads warm), not the point that matters for doneness or safety.

## How much variance is normal

For a cut probed correctly in roughly the same region — center of the thickest part, away from bone, fat seams, and the pan or grate — reading-to-reading variance of 2 to 5°F between insertions a half-inch or so apart is ordinary and reflects the gradient and tissue variation described above, not a faulty unit. Variance that shows up as consistently 8 to 10°F or more off at what should be the same spot, or a reading that won't settle and keeps drifting after 15-20 seconds of holding steady, points to something other than normal meat variation: a slow sensor, a dying battery in a digital unit, or a probe that's drifted out of calibration.

Calibration is worth checking directly rather than inferring from cooking results. An ice-water bath (a slurry of ice and water, probe suspended without touching the container) should read 32°F; boiling water at sea level should read 212°F, adjusted roughly 1°F lower for every 500 feet of elevation. Dial thermometers, which rely on a bimetallic coil, drift out of calibration more readily than digital instant-read or probe units and benefit from checking before a cook that matters, not after a reading looks suspicious.

## Where people get this wrong

The most common mistake is treating cut-to-cut variance as something perfect technique should eliminate, then blaming the thermometer when it doesn't. A close second is inserting the probe vertically into thin cuts — burgers, chops, chicken breasts — where the sensing area ends up spanning from the exterior into open air rather than sitting fully inside the meat; the fix is inserting from the side, parallel to the cooking surface, so the whole sensing length stays in tissue. Color is a separate trap: pigment changes with oxygen exposure, myoglobin content, and marinade or cure chemistry, none of which track internal temperature reliably enough to substitute for a reading.

## What this doesn't settle

Consumer probes don't have the spatial resolution to map a cut's full internal gradient, so there's no way to know, for a specific roast, exactly how many degrees of spread a given marbling pattern should produce — the 2-5°F range is a practical rule of thumb from how these probes behave in typical cuts, not a measured constant. And carryover cooking after the meat leaves heat shifts every one of these readings upward on its own, which is a separate effect from the placement and gradient issues covered here.
