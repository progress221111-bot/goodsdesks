A kid's laundry basket rarely holds one kind of stain. Grass and formula show up next to spaghetti sauce, marker, and grease from a granola bar wrapper — sometimes all in the same wash, and sometimes not discovered until the load has already been through the dryer's heat. A single bottle promising to erase all of it is making a chemistry claim it can't back up: enzymes, oxidizers, and solvents each attack a different kind of bond, and a product built for one does little against the others. There's also a separate problem in a household with mixed colors and whites — dye bleeding between garments — which isn't a stain at all and doesn't respond to any stain-removal chemistry.

## The short version

- [Puracy Laundry Stain Remover Brush](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0B726SX2G?tag=momspick1-20) — for working a treatment into a stain that already went through the dryer and set deeper than a spray can reach alone.
- [AMSY Stain Remover Cloths Laundry Bundle](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D1HDW7DF?tag=momspick1-20) — for a fresh, localized mark like a collar ring or a single grease smear, caught and treated by hand right away.
- [Enzyme Laundry Booster](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GQ6S1RJT?tag=momspick1-20) — for a family running several loads a week with recurring formula, drool, or food stains, dosed into the whole wash rather than chased one spot at a time.
- [Oil Stain Remover Spray](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DPR93VJY?tag=momspick1-20) — for grease, cooking oil, lotion, or crayon, the stain category enzymes and dye-trapping products don't touch at all.

## What actually varies here

The meaningful difference across these five listings isn't scent or bottle shape — it's what problem each is actually built to solve, and that isn't always "remove a stain." Enzymes cut apart protein and starch molecules into pieces small enough to rinse away, which is why an enzyme booster works on formula or grass but does little for cooking oil. Solvent- and surfactant-based products, including bar soap, surround oil molecules so water can carry them off, since oil doesn't dissolve in water on its own. A dye-trapping sheet does neither; it absorbs loose dye released into the wash water so it doesn't redeposit on other fabric — a color-transfer problem, not a stain. That mismatch between problem and product is the real risk in a mixed laundry pile: picking the wrong tool, or expecting one product to cover a job it was never formulated for — mistaking a color-catching sheet for a stain treatment, or using an enzyme product on a stain already baked in by dryer heat.

## Shout Color Catcher Dye-Trapping Sheets

This product doesn't remove anything from a garment — it's a sheet added to the wash that's designed, per the listing, to trap loose dye released by other items so it doesn't redeposit onto whites or lighter colors, rated for use across all water temperatures. That answers a specific, common problem: a laundry pile too mixed and too frequent to sort into separate white and color loads every time. The tradeoff is that it does nothing for the stain itself. A grass-stained sock protected by a color catcher still has grass on it after the cycle; the sheet only stops that sock's dye from turning a white shirt gray in the same load. Households expecting it to double as a stain treatment will be disappointed on both fronts.

## Puracy Laundry Stain Remover Brush

This is a brush, not a formula — vegan bristles built to work a treatment into tough, deep, and aged stains, per the listing, which puts it in a different category from the chemical products here. It doesn't break down any bond on its own; it drives whatever detergent, enzyme booster, or soap treatment is already on the fabric down into the weave, which matters most on a stain that's already dried or gone through a hot dryer cycle and set deeper than a spray reaches alone. That's the situation parents actually face more than an immediate spot treatment: the stain isn't found until laundry day, after the garment's been folded and forgotten. The tradeoff is that a brush without a paired chemical treatment is just agitation on a stain that chemistry alone already left behind.

## AMSY Stain Remover Cloths Laundry Bundle

This bundle pairs a brush with an integrated soap dispenser and two 5 oz bars of Fels Naptha soap, a surfactant-based bar historically used against grease and localized grime, per the listing. That suits one specific moment: a fresh collar ring or a single grease mark caught right away and worked by hand before the garment goes into the wash. It's less suited to several unrelated stains across multiple outfits on the same laundry day — treating a full basket bar-by-bar doesn't scale the way a wash-in booster does, and two 5 oz bars are modest next to the 2 lb powder booster here. A family using it as its only stain strategy will burn through the bars faster than expected.

## Enzyme Laundry Booster

At 2 lb and a stated 64 loads, this booster is built to go into every wash rather than get dabbed onto a single spot, which makes it the more efficient option for a family generating protein- and starch-based stains — formula, drool, food, the protein component of grass — across several loads a week rather than one at a time. It's labeled scent-free, which removes one variable parents worry about on anything a child re-wears or chews the sleeve of — though scent-free is a separate claim from residue-free, and the listing doesn't address safety with incomplete rinsing. The chemistry has a real limit: enzymes are proteins, and proteins lose their structure under sustained heat. A stain already run through a hot dryer cycle has effectively had its protein bonds set by the same kind of heat that deactivates the enzyme meant to break them apart. This booster earns its keep as ongoing prevention across a busy laundry week more than as a rescue for a stain discovered after the dryer already ran.

## Oil Stain Remover Spray

At 32 fl oz, this pre-soak spray targets oil-based stains specifically — cooking grease, lotion, salad dressing, crayon — where enzymes, soap bars, and dye-trapping sheets all do little, since oil doesn't respond to protein-breaking chemistry or a sheet designed to catch loose dye. The listing describes it as industrial strength, a genuine advantage against grease but also the reason for caution: a stronger, less-diluted formula carries a higher chance of affecting a printed design than a powder dissolved into a full basin of water. Nothing in the listing states dye-safety testing for printed fabric, so this is the product here where a hidden-seam test matters most.

## How these usually fail

Enzyme powders are moisture-sensitive once opened; humidity degrades the proteins gradually, so a container left open for months won't perform at the same scoop size as a fresh one. Bar soap wears unevenly with repeated rubbing and eventually cracks along the worn edge, and dispensers built into combination brush-and-soap tools clog with dried, hardened soap if not rinsed after use. Bristles on any scrubbing brush splay and lose stiffness with repeated hard use on dried-in stains — the point where a brush stops driving treatment into the fabric and starts smearing it around. Spray-trigger nozzles on solvent bottles are the component most likely to clog or leak after sitting unused between stains. Dye-trapping sheets are single-use, so a box that runs out mid-week quietly puts a household back into sort-by-color mode without anyone noticing until a white shirt comes out gray.

## Before you buy

None of these listings claim anything about infant or toddler skin contact, so treat every one — the enzyme booster included — as a pretreatment or wash additive finished by a complete detergent wash and rinse, not something left on a garment a child will wear or chew on directly. A full rinse is what clears leftover enzyme protein, soap residue, or solvent; skipping it because a stain looks gone isn't a use case any of these products are labeled for.

Check the care label before using a solvent spray or scrub brush on a printed graphic, and test a hidden seam first, since none of these listings state dye-safety testing for printed fabric. Count how many stained loads a household actually runs each week: a bulk enzyme booster or a box of color-catching sheets fits repeated, whole-wash use, while the brush and soap bundle suit occasional, spot-by-spot treatment. And don't expect any single product to replace sorting entirely — a color catcher reduces dye-bleed risk, per the listing, but isn't described as eliminating it, so a delicate or new dark garment still deserves its own load for the first few washes.
