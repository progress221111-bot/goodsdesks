A sealed storage bin and an open shelf are not the same moisture problem, and most advice about desiccants treats them as if they were. The question that actually matters before you drop in a silica packet or a tub of calcium chloride is whether the bin is fighting a one-time amount of trapped moisture or an ongoing supply of it — because those two situations call for completely different amounts of desiccant, and one of them barely needs any at all.

## What "sealed" actually traps

A genuinely gasketed, latch-lid tote holds a fixed volume of air. At the moment you close it, that air carries whatever humidity was in the room, plus any moisture that will migrate out of the items you packed — a slightly damp towel, a pair of shoes worn in the rain the week before, cardboard photo boxes, leather. That's the entire moisture budget. There's no faucet refilling it. Once the air inside reaches equilibrium with the packed materials, humidity inside the bin stops rising on its own, because there's no new water vapor entering a truly closed volume.

In that scenario, a desiccant isn't fighting humidity — it's absorbing a finite, one-time load, and once it does, its job is largely done until you open the bin again. This is the opposite of a basement dehumidifier, which works continuously because the room keeps exchanging air with the outside. A sealed tote should not need that.

The catch is that almost no consumer tote is actually sealed to that standard. A snap-on lid without a full perimeter gasket exchanges air with the room slowly and continuously through the gaps at the corners, so it behaves less like a closed system and more like a slow-motion open shelf — humidity keeps re-entering, just gradually. Even a well-gasketed lid isn't perfectly airtight: temperature swings between day and night or across seasons make the air inside the bin expand and contract, and that cycle pumps a small amount of outside air past the gasket each time. Opening the bin to add or remove something resets the whole calculation, because you're swapping the equilibrated air for room air again. So the honest answer to whether a desiccant does anything in a sealed bin is: yes, but its job is to mop up a one-time load, not to hold a line against ongoing humidity — and how much ongoing humidity actually gets in depends on gasket quality and how often you open the lid, not on whether the label says "airtight."

## Sizing it to the bin, not the room

Desiccant capacity is rated as a percentage of its own dry weight — silica gel typically holds somewhere in the range of a quarter to a third of its weight in absorbed moisture before it's saturated. That number lets you reason about a sealed bin even without a bin-specific chart: estimate the air volume, assume typical indoor humidity at the moment you sealed it, and you're looking at a moisture load measured in single-digit grams for a standard tote-sized volume of air. The materials you packed usually contribute far more than the air does. A packet sized for a shoebox is already oversized for the one-time load in an 18- or 27-gallon tote that's staying shut — the limiting factor isn't the packet running out, it's how often the seal gets broken and the load resets. This is also why bin-specific "how many grams do you need" tables are hard to give a single honest number for: the answer depends more on your climate and your opening habits than on the tote's volume.

## Where people get this wrong

The most common mistake is packing something that's still slightly damp — laundry that felt dry but wasn't fully cured, dishware straight from a rack — and treating the desiccant as a backstop for that. A small absorber can handle ambient humidity; it usually can't handle a genuinely wet item, and if it's undersized for that load, mold risk goes up despite the packet's presence. The second mistake is assuming a snap-lid bin without a gasket is airtight because the listing calls it that; if you can hear air move when you press on the lid, you have a slow leak, not a sealed system, and a one-time desiccant charge will eventually get overwhelmed. The third is with calcium chloride specifically: it works by deliquescing, meaning it draws moisture into itself until it turns to liquid brine, and that brine collects in a reservoir at the bottom of the product. If the bin gets tipped on its side, jostled during a move, or stored upright but on an uneven surface, that liquid can spill directly onto whatever is packed beneath it, and it's mildly corrosive to metal and can stain fabric. None of that is dangerous to check for — it just means calcium chloride wants to be checked periodically and kept upright, which silica gel doesn't require since it stays solid.

## Disposable versus rechargeable, over time

Disposable silica or calcium chloride tubs are convenient but recurring: you replace them on a schedule regardless of whether the bin actually needed a full recharge. A rechargeable silica gel product — the kind with a color-change indicator that you dry out in an oven and reuse — costs more upfront but gets reused dozens of times, so for anyone storing several bins long-term, it tends to come out cheaper across two or three years, provided you actually recharge it instead of letting it sit ignored.

## What isn't settled

There's no reliable public data on exactly how many months a given packet lasts inside a specific closed tote under real household conditions — manufacturer capacity ratings come from lab tests at controlled humidity, not from a garage that swings between seasons. How much the gasket leaks over years of reuse also isn't something you can know from a spec sheet. Treat any desiccant in a sealed bin as insurance against the packing-moment load and slow leakage, not as a guarantee, and check on it — silica changes color, calcium chloride shows liquid — rather than assuming it's still working.
