## What actually varies, and what doesn't

Every garlic press does the same basic job: force a clove through a perforated plate with lever force applied through a hinge. What varies between models, and what listings rarely spell out, is the ratio of open hole area to hopper volume, the amount of mechanical force the hinge delivers to that plate, and how rigid the plate and hopper stay under load. Those three things determine how much clove turns into usable paste versus how much sits behind, stuck against the plate or wedged in the hopper corners. Buyers tend to worry most about brand reputation or handle shape, and least about hole geometry — which is backwards, since hole geometry is the single biggest driver of how much garlic you actually get out.

## How these usually fail

Hinges are the first thing to loosen on any press in this category. The pin or rivet connecting the two handles wears slightly with repeated use, and once there's play in that joint, less of your hand force reaches the plate — so the same clove that used to press clean now leaves more behind. On stainless models, that pin is sometimes a different metal than the body, and if moisture sits in the joint between uses, it can corrode faster than the rest of the tool even though the press is marketed as rust-proof overall.

Small perforation holes are prone to clogging with fibrous pulp, especially from drier or older garlic, which has more tough cell-wall material relative to moisture. A toothpick or pick usually clears it, but that's a workaround every design in this category eventually needs, built-in cleaning tool or not — a scraper sized to pass over the plate's curve still tends to miss the corners where the hopper meets the plate. Plastic handle grips, where used, are a common point of cracking right at the pivot, since that's where stress concentrates every time you squeeze. None of this is specific to any one listing here; it's generally true of hinge-and-plate presses as a category.

## Before you buy

Measure the cloves you actually cook with, not a generic "average" clove. Grocery-store garlic heads tend to run smaller than farmers-market or specialty varieties, and a hopper sized for large cloves will let small ones shift and press unevenly — which is the exact problem the studded design is trying to solve. If you press garlic daily, weight dishwasher-safe care and hinge durability more heavily than if you press it once a week, where hand-washing isn't a real burden. If a bundle includes a peeler, check whether you already own one before paying for a duplicate. And regardless of which press you buy, keep a stiff pick or an old toothpick near it — clearing clogged holes between uses is a permanent maintenance step for this entire category, not a flaw unique to any single model.
